An ETL pipeline that validates raw data and prepares a dimensional model for analytics.

Design decision. A pipeline may need to rerun after a failed load. I used idempotent processing so a rerun can produce the same warehouse state without duplicating records. I also used two approaches to dimension updates, depending on whether reporting needed to preserve the old value.

Modeling status changes as a directed graph to examine failed visits and detect reprocessing.

Design decision. I represented statuses as nodes and transitions as directed edges. That let me inspect paths through the workflow and look for reprocessing that a count of final statuses alone could miss.

Donor prediction

Technological

Led a four-person team exploring repeat donation behavior for a nonprofit through data preparation and Random Forest modeling.

What the work taught me. The goal was to help a nonprofit understand which donors might give again. That made the timing of the data part of the modeling problem. A useful prediction has to rely on information the organization would already have when making an outreach decision.

Data preparationFeature engineeringRandom ForestTeam leadership
Explore the team repository ↗
Approach & evaluation +

Combining data for prediction

We merged donation history, demographics, and ZIP code data to explore which donors might give again. The project connected upstream data preparation with downstream model performance.

Experimental result

One notebook run reports 96.21% mean accuracy across five stratified folds and mean F1 of 0.9635. These are development results, not verified future-period performance.

Evaluation limitations

Oversampling before splitting, feature selection across the development dataset, and donation features extending beyond the prediction cutoff can inflate scores. A stronger evaluation would restrict features to past data, fit selection and oversampling within training folds, and test on a later period.

Deploying AI in an Open World: Principled and Practical OOD Detection

On October 29, I attended a guest lecture by Dr. Ganesh Sundaramoorthi, Director of Research at RTX Technology Research Center, about out-of-distribution (OOD) detection in artificial intelligence. Before attending, I didn’t know much about the topic, but I left with a much better understanding of why it matters. Although some of the math concepts were unfamiliar to me, Dr. Sundaramoorthi explained the ideas in a simple and easy way, especially through his real-world examples.

One of the main ideas I learned was that AI models are trained on a specific distribution of data, but real-world conditions do not always match what they saw during training. This creates a problem because a model might confidently make an incorrect prediction when it meets something unfamiliar. Dr. Sundaramoorthi explained how OOD detection helps identify these situations. He gave examples involving self-driving cars, cybersecurity, and aerospace systems, where recognizing unfamiliar inputs can be especially important for safety and reliability.

Another interesting part of the lecture was how existing OOD detection methods can produce false positives by treating irrelevant changes as meaningful. Dr. Sundaramoorthi discussed using information theory, including KL divergence and the information bottleneck principle, to develop a more effective approach. While I did not fully understand every equation, I understood the goal of separating useful information from irrelevant features so that models can better recognize genuinely unfamiliar data. I also found his discussion of parameter tuning interesting. Many methods depend on having examples of OOD data available during validation, which may not be realistic when the unknown situations are exactly what we are trying to detect. His proposed approach to tuning without given OOD data made me think about how difficult it can be to prepare AI systems for unpredictable environments.

As someone interested in becoming a Data and AI Engineer, this lecture helped me see another side of developing AI systems. I have worked on machine learning models before, but I usually only worry about training, evaluating accuracy, and improving performance. This lecture highlighted that getting good results on a test dataset does not guarantee a model will remain reliable after deployment. I particularly liked the connection to large language models, where OOD detection could help identify questions outside a model's knowledge or intended task rather than allowing it to produce an unreliable answer.

One question I still have is how an AI system should respond after detecting an unfamiliar input. Should it simply flag the situation, or could it eventually learn to adapt without needing to be retrained? I didn’t really disagree with anything presented, but it would have helped if he had explained a little more about how the math actually translates into a working system. Overall, I enjoyed the lecture because it introduced me to an important area of AI that I had not considered before and made me more interested in building systems that are not only accurate but also dependable in real-world situations.

Source: Guest lecture by Dr. Ganesh Sundaramoorthi, RTX Technology Research Center, October 29, 2026.
